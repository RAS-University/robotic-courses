## General Motivation

This introductory animated video presents the motivation and vision behind the IEEE RAS University Course on Sustainability Robotics. It explores how the rapid growth of robotics and intelligent technologies is transforming society while also creating important environmental challenges, including rising energy consumption and the global increase of electronic waste. Starting from recent data on e-waste production, the video highlights the urgent need to rethink how robots and technological systems are designed, manufactured, used, and disposed of.

The introduction explains the concept of Sustainability Robotics as an emerging interdisciplinary field that combines sustainable robot design with the use of robotic technologies to address environmental and societal challenges. The video introduces key themes of the course, including recyclable and biodegradable materials, energy-efficient systems, life-cycle assessment, circular design strategies, self-healing technologies, and environmentally responsible manufacturing. It also presents real-world applications where robots can contribute to sustainability, such as ecosystem monitoring, precision agriculture, disaster response, and operations in extreme environments.

Through a forward-looking perspective, the animation envisions a future in which robots coexist with natural ecosystems in a regenerative and responsible way — using clean energy, repairing themselves, supporting biodiversity, and biodegrading safely at the end of their operational life. The introduction sets the stage for the course by encouraging students and researchers to develop a new generation of robotics technologies that are not only high-performing and intelligent, but also environmentally and socially sustainable.

> 📖 **Reading lesson:** The 5 Levels of Sustainable Robotics

This lesson introduces the concept of the "Sustainability Levels for Robotics," a framework that helps classify how robotics technologies contribute to environmental sustainability. Inspired by the idea of autonomy levels in self-driving vehicles, the framework defines five progressive levels of sustainable robotics, ranging from robots that improve existing recycling processes to future systems capable of self-powering, self-repair, and circular operation. The lecture explores how sustainability in robotics must go beyond applications alone and include the full life cycle of robotic systems, including materials, energy consumption, manufacturing, maintenance, and end-of-life strategies.

This lesson presents a structured framework for understanding sustainability in robotics through five progressive levels of development. The lecture begins by discussing the growing role of robotics in addressing global sustainability challenges and supporting the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). At the same time, it highlights the importance of evaluating the environmental impact of robotics itself, including energy use, raw materials, manufacturing processes, logistics, and electronic waste generation.
The lecture then introduces the five levels of sustainable robotics. Level 1 focuses on robots that improve existing sustainable practices such as waste sorting, recycling, environmental monitoring, and renewable energy maintenance. Level 2 expands this idea by showing how robotics can enable entirely new forms of recycling and material recovery that were previously impossible or economically unfeasible. Level 3 examines the transition toward electrified and autonomous robotic systems, particularly in transportation, logistics, and agriculture, where robotics can reduce emissions, optimize resource use, and support precision operations.
The lesson continues with Level 4, which addresses sustainability directly within robot design and manufacturing. Topics include local production, additive manufacturing, recyclable materials, modularity, and the reuse of electronic components. Finally, Level 5 explores future visions of robotics involving self-powered, self-repairing, and self-recycling systems inspired by biological processes and circular economy principles. Examples include solar-powered robots, energy-harvesting systems, modular robotics, and robots capable of extended autonomous operation in remote environments.
Throughout the lecture, students are encouraged to think critically about sustainability not only as an application domain for robotics, but also as a design philosophy that affects every stage of a robot's life cycle.

**Main topics covered:**
- Introduction to sustainability in robotics and the UN Sustainable Development Goals
- Environmental impacts of robotics across the supply chain
- Externalities of robotic technologies: energy, materials, logistics, and waste
- The five levels of sustainable robotics
- Level 1: Robots improving existing recycling and sustainability processes
- Level 2: Robots enabling new recycling and material recovery methods
- Level 3: Electrification and autonomous sustainable mobility and agriculture
- Precision agriculture and small autonomous farming robots
- Level 4: Sustainable robot manufacturing and circular design
- Recyclable materials, local production, and modular robotics
- Reuse of electronic waste and "garbatrage" design concepts
- Level 5: Self-powering, self-repairing, and self-recycling robots
- Solar-powered and energy-harvesting robotic systems
- Circular economy principles in robotics
- Future visions of regenerative and environmentally integrated robots

> **Take-home message:** Sustainability in robotics is not limited to using robots for environmental applications; it also requires redesigning robotic systems themselves to minimize environmental impact throughout their entire life cycle. The five sustainability levels provide a framework for understanding how robotics can evolve from improving existing sustainable processes to becoming fully regenerative systems capable of self-powering, self-repair, and circular material use. The future of robotics will depend not only on intelligence and performance, but also on the ability to create technologies that operate responsibly within ecological and societal boundaries.

This lesson explores the emerging field of biodegradable and transient electronics for sustainable robotics. It explains how conventional electronic materials contribute to growing e-waste problems and introduces biodegradable alternatives that can safely dissolve or decompose after use. The lecture presents the scientific foundations of transient electronics, including dissolvable silicon and biodegradable electronic materials, alongside real-world examples of soft robots, sensors, and robotic systems designed for environmentally responsible end-of-life disposal. Electronics are essential components of robotic systems, functioning similarly to organs in the human body: sensors act as senses, processors act as the brain, power systems provide energy like the heart, and communication systems resemble neural networks. The lecture introduces biodegradable and transient electronics as a sustainable alternative for future robotic systems. Particular attention is given to the discovery that ultra-thin silicon structures can dissolve in water under physiological conditions, enabling fully transient electronic systems.

Several examples of biodegradable robotic systems are presented, including gliding environmental monitoring robots, soft grippers, and multifunctional biodegradable robotic fingers capable of sensing, heating, stimulation, and drug delivery.
The lecture concludes by emphasizing that sustainable robotics must consider the entire lifecycle of robotic systems, including safe degradation, material recovery, and environmentally responsible disposal.

### Sustainability Robotics. Animation

Robotics is advancing rapidly, creating more and more opportunities to support our daily lives and to assist in critical situations. But as machines become smarter and more capable, their increased presence in our world creates challenges.

Modern technologies often require large amounts of energy and generate significant electronic waste, or “e-waste,” at the end of their lifecycle. 
According to the UN’s Global E-waste Monitor 2024, the world produced about 62 billion kilograms of e-waste by 2022 — and that figure is growing five times faster than recycling capacity. These numbers remind us that progress has a cost. And that we need to rethink how we design, build, and use technology to minimize its impact on the environment.
This is where sustainability in robotics becomes essential.

How can we create machines that serve people while also respecting the planet? To make robotics truly sustainable, we must consider every stage of a robot’s life: the choice of materials and energy sources, manufacturing, operation, and end-of-life management. Environmental ethics, economics, and social impact all play a role in shaping these new approaches.

At the core of this vision lies Sustainability Robotics, an emerging field that combines two key perspectives: Sustainable Robot Design – reducing environmental impact through recyclable or biodegradable materials, energy-efficient systems, and eco-friendly production; and Robotic Solutions for Sustainability Challenges – using robots to tackle environmental, social, and economic issues such as ecosystem monitoring, disaster response, and precision agriculture.

The IEEE RAS University Course on Sustainability Robotics offers an interdisciplinary journey into this new mindset. It connects robotics, ecology, materials science, environmental studies, design, and sustainable development, integrating both ethical and economic aspects. 

The goal is to prepare a new generation of engineers ready to design responsible,nlong-lasting technologies. Topics include nsustainability principles, life-cycle assessment (LCA), biodegradable materials and electronics,nself-healing robots, nand circular design strategies. 

Students and researchers will also explore how robotics can address sustainability challenges — from environmental monitoring on land, underwater, and in the air, to agriculture, disaster recovery, and extreme environments.

Looking ahead, the vision is of a future where machines and nature coexist in a regenerative cycle.  Robots will evolve into systems that collaborating with ecosystems — cleaning oceans, restoring soils, or maintaining biodiversity harvest clean energy and repair themselves.

Biodegrade when their mission ends. Advances in bio-inspired design, soft materials, and circular manufacturing will enable a new generation of sustainable robots — technology that supports, rather than consumes, the planet’s life systems.

### Introduction to Sustainability Robotics. Barbara Mazzolai, IIT

I'm very pleased to start this new course on Sustainability Robotics, supported by the IEEE Robotics and Automation Society.

In this lesson, I will introduce what Sustainability Robotics actually means and why it has become so urgent to educate a new generation of professionals in this emerging research field.

Sustainability Robotics is a new field structured around two key dimensions.

First, sustainable robot design focuses on developing robots that minimize environmental impact by using biodegradable or reusable materials, innovative energy sources, and eco-friendly manufacturing processes.

Second, robotic solutions for sustainability challenges involve leveraging robotics to address environmental, social, and economic issues.

We are proposing two complementary approaches: developing robots that are more sustainable, but also using robots for sustainability. These robots can open new application scenarios, such as waste management, precision agriculture, climate monitoring, and renewable energy.

To deeply understand this approach, we need to return to the origin of the term “sustainability.”

In 1987, Gro Harlem Brundtland, the first woman Prime Minister of Norway and former Director-General of the World Health Organization, defined sustainable development in the Our Common Future report for the World Commission on Environment and Development. She defined it as “development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”

This apparently simple definition completely changed how we address the relationship between environment, development, and human well-being.

Promoting sustainability also means protecting biodiversity. There is a clear relationship between climate change, poverty, and biodiversity loss.

Since 1997, there has been an average decline of nearly 70% in populations of vertebrates such as mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, and amphibians. We know very little about invertebrates, which represent 95% of animal species, and we have very limited data on other kingdoms such as bacteria, fungi, protists, and plants.

What is the role of technology in this context?

On one hand, technology has deeply improved our quality of life. On the other hand, it has contributed to environmental and health challenges.

In 2022 alone, the world generated a record high of 62 million tons of electronic waste produced by smartphones, computers, and other electronic devices, representing an 82% increase compared to 2010. Projections suggest that we will reach a production of 82 million tons per year by 2030.

Scientists and technology developers must minimize this production and reverse this trend.

One key idea is to introduce the concept of a life cycle in robotics by mimicking nature. We have to consider the potential impact of our robots from the beginning until the end of their life cycle.

The next generation of robots will adapt morphologically and behaviorally to dynamic conditions and be increasingly integrated into natural and human environments. When their task ends, they need to be reusable, recyclable, or biodegradable.

These robots will be able to grow, evolve, learn, show social intelligence, and even morph or change shape.

To make robotics greener, we need a completely different paradigm, starting from materials, such as biodegradable and self-healing materials with functionalized sensing capabilities.

We also need new forms of edible and biodegradable electronics, energy harvesting from the environment, and new design tools to minimize energy consumption and e-waste.

By mimicking nature, robots can protect biodiversity and improve sustainability.

As mentioned, biodegradable soft robots inspired by plant structures such as drilling and flying seeds (for example, Pelargonium or Avena) offer zero e-waste solutions. These structures, although dead and metabolically inert, produce movement based on their cellulose matrices and lignin microfibers reacting to humidity and temperature variations.

The robots use biodegradable fluorescent or colorimetric sensors instead of electronics or batteries and can be released in huge quantities by drones for precision agriculture and reforestation.

This field requires a new “renaissance engineer” who combines high technical skills with adaptability, creativity, and the ability to work in multidisciplinary environments.

Sustainability Robotics is a responsibility, offering a science-based, bio-inspired approach to fully integrate robots into our ecosystems through a holistic life-cycle approach.


### Edible batteries. Mario Caironi, IIT

While energy harvesting methods such as solar and thermal energy are well developed, sustainable energy storage remains of utmost importance because energy availability is not continuous.

Batteries provide defined operating voltages and high energy density, but they rely on mined and potentially toxic materials, creating e-waste issues and significant hazards.

This toxicity is a major barrier for emerging applications such as ingestible electronics and smart pills designed to monitor gastrointestinal health.

Standard silver-oxide button cells are hazardous. When exposed to aqueous fluids, they produce hydrogen gas and can severely damage internal tissues.

To address this challenge, we are developing electronics made entirely from food-derived materials to create perfectly safe smart pills that leave no toxic waste.

These technologies could also be used in agrifood tags to prevent counterfeiting and provide power for future edible soft robots used in wildlife rescue and medication delivery.

In 2023, we demonstrated the first edible electrochemical battery.

The cathode is made from riboflavin, the anode from quercetin, and the conductive filler from activated carbon combined with a binder. The separator consists of nori seaweed soaked in sodium hydrogen sulfate, while edible gold serves as the current collector.

Because of the properties of these molecules, the battery operates at a safe voltage of 0.65 V, well below the water electrolysis threshold of 1.23 V, completely preventing internal burning.

Recent laboratory prototypes have achieved higher mass loading comparable to commercial batteries, exceeding 0.1 mAh of capacity.

We also collaborated with culinary professionals to replace beeswax encapsulation with fine chocolate and demonstrated these functional batteries at the World Expo.

While electrochemical batteries are suitable for low-power sensors and IoT devices, they do not provide sufficient power for robotic actuation.

For this reason, alternative concepts such as the edible pneumatic battery have been developed.

This two-compartment battery separates citric acid and sodium bicarbonate. When activated—for example, by a wild animal stepping on it—the chemicals mix and release carbon dioxide gas.

The gas inflates an edible gelatin actuator, triggering repetitive movements that mimic prey and deliver medication to wild animals.

Ultimately, edible energy storage addresses both toxicity and e-waste challenges in ingestible technologies while providing sustainable solutions for emerging robotic applications.

### Electronics. Seung-Kyun Kang, Seoul University

Electronics play a critical role in robots, functioning much like human organs. Sensors act as senses, processors act as the brain, power supplies act as the heart, and communication pathways act as synapses.

However, conventional electronics often contain toxic materials such as lead and cadmium, contributing to a massive global e-waste crisis, with approximately 62 million tons produced each year.

To reduce this impact, biodegradable pathways allow electronics to decompose safely after use.

As soft robotics advances, it creates additional recycling challenges due to the use of thermoset elastomers and complex multilayer composites.

Transient electronics provide an alternative: electronic systems designed to disappear through dissolution, degradation, or disintegration, safely returning to the environment.

A key breakthrough was the discovery that nanoscale silicon, such as 100-nanometer silicon ribbons, can dissolve in water within a practical timeframe, unlike bulk silicon.

This discovery enabled fully biodegradable electronic systems, including capacitors, inductors, transistors, and diodes, while leveraging conventional semiconductor fabrication processes.

Researchers have demonstrated partially degradable glider robots using lightweight cryogel frames and carbon-based sensors to monitor environmental conditions before safely degrading.

They have also developed fully water-soluble pneumatic grippers using biodegradable PLCL polymers combined with stretchable PEDOT electrodes.

Important challenges remain, including ensuring durability and multifunctionality while maintaining degradability.

A recent study addressed these challenges by creating a hyper-durable robotic finger made from biodegradable PGS and inorganic electronics based on magnesium, molybdenum, and silicon.

The system successfully withstood more than one million operating cycles while integrating sensing, heating, and drug-delivery functions.

Ultimately, all components decomposed in a controlled composting environment, and the resulting degradation byproducts did not negatively affect subsequent plant growth.

Sustainable robotics must look beyond operational performance and embrace a circular life cycle in which robots are environmentally responsible from creation to their sustainable end of life.

### Actuators. Vicky Webster-Wood, Carnegie Mellon University

This lecture focuses on sustainable actuators, the components that enable robots to move.

Conventional actuators, such as electric motors and pneumatic systems, rely heavily on non-renewable materials, contributing to e-waste and microplastic pollution.

Alternatively, sustainable materials can be harvested locally. Examples include seaweeds for alginate gels, crab exoskeletons for chitin, plant materials for cellulose, and even bacteria or fungi.

Sustainable actuators broadly fall into two categories: non-living bio-derived materials and living biomaterials.

Non-living materials, such as gelatin derived from food industry byproducts, are widely used. Gelatin can serve as a stretchable electrode by being patterned onto a biodegradable balloon that contracts under an electric field. It can also form the structural body of a biodegradable artificial muscle.

Plant-derived fibers, such as those obtained from konjac roots, can be cast into soft three-dimensional grippers.

Using these materials helps prevent the release of microplastics during interaction with the environment.

Alginate, a soft gel derived from brown seaweed, can be manufactured using embedded printing techniques such as the FRESH method.

In this process, alginate ink is 3D printed into a calcium chloride-doped gelatin support bath, where it solidifies into a stable gel structure capable of forming hydraulic bending and linear actuators.

Because alginate is naturally biodegradable and safely edible by marine organisms, these actuators can be deployed in sensitive aquatic ecosystems.

The second category consists of biohybrid robots that use living tissues for actuation.

Researchers cultivate skeletal muscle tissue by casting cells into molds containing growth media. The compacted tissue is then transferred onto a compliant robotic skeleton.

By switching to differentiation media, the cells mature. Around day six they begin spontaneous contractions, and by day fourteen they can be reliably controlled through electrical stimulation.

Despite current challenges related to durability, device variability, and manufacturing scalability, sustainable actuators offer tremendous potential.

They are naturally biocompatible and support a circular product life cycle, enabling future robots to safely degrade, monitor ecosystems, and perform agricultural tasks without introducing harmful electronic waste into the environment.

### Control, AI & data. Egidio Falotico, SSSA

Today, I will focus on emerging sustainable technologies for robotics across three key elements: control, artificial intelligence, and data.

Control is how a robot decides and executes actions in the physical world, moving from classical model-based approaches toward methods that integrate AI.

In complex and unstructured environments, learning-based approaches shift the burden from system identification to data, enabling reinforcement learning for full-body coordination.

Another approach relies on vision-language-action models, which learn a shared latent space linking visual features, language instructions, and action representations. Instead of explicitly programming tasks, we provide high-level instructions, and the model maps them directly into actions.

However, deploying robots in the real world introduces significant challenges. Hardware degrades, environments change, computational resources are limited, and data becomes non-stationary.

Under these resource constraints, learning must be an ongoing process rather than a one-time event.

We must address these challenges by optimizing data representation through event-based coding, using continual learning algorithms, and adopting hardware such as neuromorphic chips and low-power edge accelerators.

Current vision-language-action models are computationally expensive and require large datasets and substantial inference power, which often conflicts with sustainability requirements.

Instead of training a model once and then freezing it, continual learning allows a model to progressively acquire new knowledge from a stream of experiences without retraining from scratch and without forgetting previous knowledge.

This avoids the computational and energy costs associated with repeatedly rebuilding datasets and relying on cloud-based retraining.

Continual learning methods include replay strategies, generative methods, regularization techniques that constrain parameter updates, and architectural methods that modify model structure. Each approach balances memory requirements, computation, and flexibility.

Continual learning applies to class-incremental scenarios, domain-incremental scenarios, and task-incremental scenarios.

In summary, static training is insufficient for non-stationary robotic environments. Sustainable learning must be resource-aware, and continual learning enables the adaptation required over time without retraining from scratch.


### Sustainability applications of robotic systems: underwater robotics. Cecilia Laschi, National University of Singapore

Today, we take a small journey into underwater robotics from a sustainability perspective.

The oceans are crucial for our planet. They host life, regulate the climate, produce food and energy, and are important for trade and tourism. Despite their importance, they remain largely unexplored.

Robots typically navigate the pelagic zone, or water column. However, it is very interesting to operate in the benthic zone, the sea bottom, because it hosts 98% of marine species, experiences human activities, and is where pollution concentrates.

We can learn from benthic species such as the octopus, which possesses remarkable abilities for walking, swimming, and manipulation.

The octopus simplifies its control mechanisms through a positive interaction with water. During walking, its legs shorten and elongate to reduce drag. Swimming is produced through jet propulsion by contracting the mantle. Propulsion is therefore generated by the morphology and mechanical properties of the body.

This form of underwater leg locomotion produces self-stabilizing gaits and can be replicated in robots using simple controls and mechanical compliance.

For instance, a robot can swim via jet propulsion using a single motor to contract the mantle, while another uses the anisotropic stiffness of its arms—stiff during the stroke and soft during recovery—to reduce drag.

However, traditional robots use materials, electronics, and metals that are not always compatible with marine environments and can themselves become pollutants.

For this reason, we are exploring electronics-free soft robots based on fluidic actuation. Instead of traditional electronic valves, these systems use a bistable fluidic valve with only two states.

When attached to a 3D actuator and an air supply, this system generates continuous rhythmic pressure oscillations. These simple oscillations can produce natural rhythmic movements such as walking or swimming.

All components can potentially be replaced with biodegradable materials.

In this way, we can envision sustainable future robots operating in the benthic zone for oceanography, ecology, and monitoring human activities, with programmable lifespans and controlled material degradation.
