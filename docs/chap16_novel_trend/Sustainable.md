## General Motivation

This introductory animated video presents the motivation and vision behind the IEEE RAS University Course on Sustainability Robotics. It explores how the rapid growth of robotics and intelligent technologies is transforming society while also creating important environmental challenges, including rising energy consumption and the global increase of electronic waste. Starting from recent data on e-waste production, the video highlights the urgent need to rethink how robots and technological systems are designed, manufactured, used, and disposed of.

The introduction explains the concept of Sustainability Robotics as an emerging interdisciplinary field that combines sustainable robot design with the use of robotic technologies to address environmental and societal challenges. The video introduces key themes of the course, including recyclable and biodegradable materials, energy-efficient systems, life-cycle assessment, circular design strategies, self-healing technologies, and environmentally responsible manufacturing. It also presents real-world applications where robots can contribute to sustainability, such as ecosystem monitoring, precision agriculture, disaster response, and operations in extreme environments.

Through a forward-looking perspective, the animation envisions a future in which robots coexist with natural ecosystems in a regenerative and responsible way — using clean energy, repairing themselves, supporting biodiversity, and biodegrading safely at the end of their operational life. The introduction sets the stage for the course by encouraging students and researchers to develop a new generation of robotics technologies that are not only high-performing and intelligent, but also environmentally and socially sustainable.

#### Sustainability Levels for Robotics
*Andra Keay, Silicon Valley Robotics*

> - Five progressive levels of sustainable robotics: a structured framework
> - Environmental impacts of robotics across the supply chain
> - Robots improving existing sustainability processes (Level 1) to self-powering, self-repairing systems (Level 5)
> - Sustainable robot manufacturing, circular design, and garbatrage concepts
> - Circular economy principles in robotics

> 📖 **Reading lesson:** The 5 Levels of Sustainable Robotics

This lesson introduces the concept of the "Sustainability Levels for Robotics," a framework that helps classify how robotics technologies contribute to environmental sustainability. Inspired by the idea of autonomy levels in self-driving vehicles, the framework defines five progressive levels of sustainable robotics, ranging from robots that improve existing recycling processes to future systems capable of self-powering, self-repair, and circular operation. The lecture explores how sustainability in robotics must go beyond applications alone and include the full life cycle of robotic systems, including materials, energy consumption, manufacturing, maintenance, and end-of-life strategies.

This lesson presents a structured framework for understanding sustainability in robotics through five progressive levels of development. The lecture begins by discussing the growing role of robotics in addressing global sustainability challenges and supporting the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). At the same time, it highlights the importance of evaluating the environmental impact of robotics itself, including energy use, raw materials, manufacturing processes, logistics, and electronic waste generation.
The lecture then introduces the five levels of sustainable robotics. Level 1 focuses on robots that improve existing sustainable practices such as waste sorting, recycling, environmental monitoring, and renewable energy maintenance. Level 2 expands this idea by showing how robotics can enable entirely new forms of recycling and material recovery that were previously impossible or economically unfeasible. Level 3 examines the transition toward electrified and autonomous robotic systems, particularly in transportation, logistics, and agriculture, where robotics can reduce emissions, optimize resource use, and support precision operations.
The lesson continues with Level 4, which addresses sustainability directly within robot design and manufacturing. Topics include local production, additive manufacturing, recyclable materials, modularity, and the reuse of electronic components. Finally, Level 5 explores future visions of robotics involving self-powered, self-repairing, and self-recycling systems inspired by biological processes and circular economy principles. Examples include solar-powered robots, energy-harvesting systems, modular robotics, and robots capable of extended autonomous operation in remote environments.
Throughout the lecture, students are encouraged to think critically about sustainability not only as an application domain for robotics, but also as a design philosophy that affects every stage of a robot's life cycle.

**Main topics covered:**
- Introduction to sustainability in robotics and the UN Sustainable Development Goals
- Environmental impacts of robotics across the supply chain
- Externalities of robotic technologies: energy, materials, logistics, and waste
- The five levels of sustainable robotics
- Level 1: Robots improving existing recycling and sustainability processes
- Level 2: Robots enabling new recycling and material recovery methods
- Level 3: Electrification and autonomous sustainable mobility and agriculture
- Precision agriculture and small autonomous farming robots
- Level 4: Sustainable robot manufacturing and circular design
- Recyclable materials, local production, and modular robotics
- Reuse of electronic waste and "garbatrage" design concepts
- Level 5: Self-powering, self-repairing, and self-recycling robots
- Solar-powered and energy-harvesting robotic systems
- Circular economy principles in robotics
- Future visions of regenerative and environmentally integrated robots

> **Take-home message:** Sustainability in robotics is not limited to using robots for environmental applications; it also requires redesigning robotic systems themselves to minimize environmental impact throughout their entire life cycle. The five sustainability levels provide a framework for understanding how robotics can evolve from improving existing sustainable processes to becoming fully regenerative systems capable of self-powering, self-repair, and circular material use. The future of robotics will depend not only on intelligence and performance, but also on the ability to create technologies that operate responsibly within ecological and societal boundaries.

#### Biodegradable Electronics
*Seung-Kyun Kang, Seoul University*

> - Environmental impact of conventional electronics and e-waste
> - Transient electronics: dissolvable silicon and biodegradable materials
> - Biodegradable capacitors, resistors, transistors, and wireless devices
> - Biodegradable glider robots, soft grippers, and multifunctional robotic systems
> - Circular lifecycle approaches and compostable robotic devices

![Biodegradable Electronics](https://youtu.be/VxYdhLghPgI?si=ojXzk0Mtj5RLlxLT)
><sub>Electronics. Seung-Kyun Kang, Seoul University. YouTube video. Available at: https://youtu.be/VxYdhLghPgI?si=ojXzk0Mtj5RLlxLT</sub>

This lesson explores the emerging field of biodegradable and transient electronics for sustainable robotics. It explains how conventional electronic materials contribute to growing e-waste problems and introduces biodegradable alternatives that can safely dissolve or decompose after use. The lecture presents the scientific foundations of transient electronics, including dissolvable silicon and biodegradable electronic materials, alongside real-world examples of soft robots, sensors, and robotic systems designed for environmentally responsible end-of-life disposal. The lecture also discusses the remaining technical challenges related to durability, multifunctionality, and circular material lifecycles.

Electronics are essential components of robotic systems, functioning similarly to organs in the human body: sensors act as senses, processors act as the brain, power systems provide energy like the heart, and communication systems resemble neural networks. However, traditional electronics rely heavily on materials such as lead and cadmium, which create environmental and health risks when discarded.
The lecture introduces biodegradable and transient electronics as a sustainable alternative for future robotic systems. These systems are designed to operate reliably during use and then intentionally dissolve, degrade, or disintegrate after their functional lifetime without leaving harmful waste behind. Particular attention is given to the discovery that ultra-thin silicon structures can dissolve in water under physiological conditions, enabling fully transient electronic systems.
The lecture further explores the development of biodegradable semiconductors, metals, dielectrics, and polymers that can be integrated into conventional electronic components such as capacitors, resistors, transistors, and wireless stimulators. Several examples of biodegradable robotic systems are presented, including gliding environmental monitoring robots, soft grippers, and multifunctional biodegradable robotic fingers capable of sensing, heating, stimulation, and drug delivery.
The lecture concludes by emphasizing that sustainable robotics must consider the entire lifecycle of robotic systems, including safe degradation, material recovery, and environmentally responsible disposal.

**Main topics covered:**
- Role of electronics in robotic systems
- Environmental impact of conventional electronics and e-waste
- Reuse, repair, recycling, and biodegradation strategies
- Definition and principles of transient electronics
- Discovery of dissolvable silicon technology
- Development of biodegradable electronic materials
- Biodegradable capacitors, resistors, transistors, and diodes
- Wafer-scale transient electronic systems
- Wireless biodegradable electronic devices
- Biodegradable glider robots for environmental monitoring
- Soft robotic grippers with integrated sensing
- Water-soluble robotic platforms
- Challenges of durability and reliability
- Multifunctional biodegradable robotic systems
- Circular lifecycle approaches for robotics materials
- Compostable robotic devices and environmental safety

> **Take-home message:** Biodegradable and transient electronics represent a major step toward truly sustainable robotics. Rather than focusing only on robotic performance during operation, this approach considers the entire lifecycle of robotic systems, including their environmental impact after disposal. By combining biodegradable materials, dissolvable electronics, and circular lifecycle strategies, future robots may become high-performance systems that can safely return to nature or re-enter industrial material cycles at the end of their use.

> - Embedded 3D printing and hydraulic biodegradable robotic grippers
> - Biohybrid robotics using living skeletal muscle tissue

![Sustainable Actuators](https://youtu.be/qIFkt-VX398?si=C7QJWmGAI6M7Q-D3)
><sub>Actuators. Vicky Webster-Wood, Carnegie Mellon University. YouTube video. Available at: https://youtu.be/qIFkt-VX398?si=C7QJWmGAI6M7Q-D3</sub>

This lesson examines sustainable actuators for robotics, focusing on environmentally friendly alternatives to conventional motors, hydraulics, and pneumatic systems. It introduces biodegradable and biohybrid materials derived from natural sources such as seaweed, gelatin, chitin, plants, bacteria, and fungi. The lecture presents examples of sustainable actuator fabrication, including gelatin-based artificial muscles, alginate soft actuators, and biohybrid robotic systems powered by living muscle tissue. It also discusses the opportunities and challenges of creating robotic systems that are biodegradable, biocompatible, and suitable for environmentally sensitive applications.

Actuators are the components that enable robotic movement, converting energy into motion through mechanisms such as electromagnetism, pressure, heat, or electricity. Traditional actuators are typically made from non-renewable materials and synthetic polymers that are difficult to recycle and contribute to electronic waste and environmental pollution.
The lecture introduces sustainable actuators as an alternative approach that uses biodegradable and biologically derived materials. These materials can be sourced from marine organisms, plants, agricultural by-products, and even living cells. Examples include gelatin derived from collagen, alginate extracted from brown seaweed, chitin from crustacean shells, and cellulose-based plant materials.
Different fabrication methods for sustainable actuators are explored, including casting, extrusion, and advanced 3D printing techniques. The lecture presents examples of biodegradable grippers, artificial muscles, hydraulic soft actuators, and multi-degree-of-freedom robotic arms built from natural materials.
The lecture also introduces biohybrid robotics, where living tissues such as skeletal muscle cells are directly integrated into robotic systems to produce movement. These living actuators can self-organize, mature, and respond to electrical stimulation, enabling controllable robotic motion.

Finally, the lecture discusses the main challenges facing sustainable actuators, including durability, environmental sensitivity, manufacturing scalability, and performance limitations, while emphasizing their potential for environmentally responsible robotics applications.

- Embedded 3D printing techniques for soft actuators
- Hydraulic biodegradable actuators and robotic grippers
- Biocompatibility and environmental safety of alginate systems
- Biohybrid robotics using living materials
- Microorganism-based robots and tissue-powered robots
- Skeletal muscle tissue as a robotic actuator
- Muscle growth, differentiation, and electrical stimulation
- Modular assembly of biohybrid robotic systems
- Challenges of sustainable actuators, including durability and scalability
- Environmental limitations of biodegradable materials
- Circular lifecycle approaches in robotics
- Applications in ecosystem monitoring, agriculture, and conservation

> **Take-home message:** Sustainable actuators offer a promising path toward environmentally responsible robotics by replacing conventional synthetic materials with biodegradable and biologically derived alternatives. Through the integration of natural materials and living tissues, future robotic systems may become more compatible with ecosystems, reduce e-waste and microplastic pollution, and support sustainable applications in agriculture, conservation, and environmental monitoring.
