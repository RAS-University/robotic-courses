## Basics of Geometric Algebra

### Definitions

Geometric algebras are a family of algebras that are defined over vector spaces to allow operations with subspaces. Geometric algebra is also known as Clifford algebra. In general, Clifford algebras can be defined for vector spaces over arbitrary fields, e.g. also over the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, but usually geometric algebra refers to a Clifford algebra that is generated by a vector space over the field of the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$. Here, we only consider this case and first give some basic definitions. This introduction is not exhaustive and only touches on the surface of the most important concepts. For thorough introductions the books by Perwass (2009), Lounesto (2006), and Doran (2003) are recommended.

Let $(\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r}, g)$ denote a quadratic space, i.e. a $(p+q+r)$-dimensional vector space $\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r}$ with a quadratic form $g$ defined as $g : \mathbb{R}^{p,q,r} \times \mathbb{R}^{p,q,r} \to \mathbb{R}$. Here, $p$, $q$, and $r$ are the number of basis vectors that square to 1, -1, and 0, respectively. Formally, a geometric algebra $\ga{p,q,r}$ is then defined as an associative algebra over the quadratic space $(\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r}, g)$ that forms a ring. The field $\mathbb{R}$ and the vector space $\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r}$ can be regarded as subspaces of $\ga{p,q,r}$. A general element $X \in \ga{p,q,r}$ is called a multivector. Given two multivectors $X,Y \in \ga{p,q,r}$ their algebraic product is denoted by juxtaposition, i.e. $XY$, and is called the *geometric product*.

{% include gafro_scene.html slug="signature-unit-sets" %}

Squaring a vector $\bm{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{p,q,r} \subset \ga{p,q,r}$ using the geometric product yields a scalar, i.e. $\bm{x}\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}$. This property separates geometric algebra from other associative algebras. Given two vectors $\bm{x}\_1,\bm{x}\_2 \in \ga{p,q,r}$, their geometric product can be decomposed into a symmetric and an anti-symmetric part

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}_1\bm{x}_2 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \bm{x_1}\bm{x_2} + \bm{x_2} \bm{x_1} \right) + \frac{1}{2} \left( \bm{x_1}\bm{x_2} - \bm{x_2} \bm{x_1} \right) = \bm{x}_1\inner\bm{x}_2 + \bm{x}_1\outer\bm{x}_2
\end{equation}
</div>

The symmetric part is the *inner product* of vectors

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}_1 \inner \bm{x}_2 \triangleq \frac{1}{2} \left( \bm{x_1}\bm{x_2} + \bm{x_2} \bm{x_1} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( (\bm{x}_1 + \bm{x}_2)^2 - \bm{x}_1^2 - \bm{x}_2^2 \right).
\end{equation}
</div>

The anti-symmetric part is the *outer product* of vectors

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}_1 \outer \bm{x}_2 \triangleq \frac{1}{2} \left( \bm{x_1}\bm{x_2} - \bm{x_2} \bm{x_1} \right).
\end{equation}
</div>

The inner product is related to the metric of the algebra, whereas the outer product spans vectors to $k$-vectors, where $k$ refers to the number of linearly independent basis vectors. For example, the quantity $X\_{12} = \bm{x}\_1 \outer \bm{x}\_2$ is called a bivector.

Denoting the set of basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r}$ as $\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{p,q,r}$ and considering the geometric product of the basis vector $\gae{i} \in \bar{\mathbb{R}}^{p,q,r}$, all possible combinations form the algebraic basis $\bar{\mathbb{G}}\_{p,q,r}$ of $\ga{p,q,r}$. Consequently, this basis then has $2^{p+q+r}$ elements which are called basis blades. Let $\mathbb{E} \subset \bar{\mathbb{R}}^{p,q,r}$, then $\gae{\mathbb{E}}$ denotes the basis blade

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}
    \gae{\mathbb{E}} \triangleq \bigwedge_{k=1}^{\vert \mathbb{E}\vert } \mathbb{E}\left[ k \right].
\end{equation}
</div>

For example, if $\mathbb{E} = (\gae{1},\gae{2},\gae{3})$, then $\gae{\mathbb{E}} = \gae{1} \outer \gae{2} \outer \gae{3}$ and by convention it is written as $\gae{123}$. A general multivector $X \in \ga{p,q,r}$ then is the linear combination of basis blades. As a vector space (not as an algebra) $\ga{p,q,r}$ is isomorphic to the exterior algebra $\bigwedge^{p,q,r}$, meaning that it is a graded algebra. Here, the *grade* of a basis blade $\gae{\mathbb{E}} \in \ga{p,q,r}$ with $\mathbb{E} \subset \bar{\mathbb{R}}^{p,q,r}$ is defined as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}
    \text{gr}(\gae{\mathbb{E}}) = \vert \mathbb{E}\vert ,
\end{equation}
</div>

i.e. the grade refers to the number of basis vectors in a blade. The highest grade basis blade is called the pseudoscalar, and is denoted as $I$.

Let $\gae{i} \triangleq \bar{\mathbb{G}}\_{p,q,r}\left[ i \right]$ then the *grade projection* is defined as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grade_projection}
    \grade{\gae{i}}{k} =
    \begin{cases}
        \gae{i}, \hspace{3mm} \text{gr}(\gae{i}) = k
        \\
        0, \hspace{3mm} \text{gr}(\gae{i}) \neq k
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation}
</div>

Given this definition, scalars are homogeneous multivectors of grade 0, i.e. $\mathbb{R} = \grade{\ga{p,q,r}}{0}$, and vectors are homogeneous multivectors of grade 1, i.e. $\mathbb{R}^{p,q,r} = \grade{\ga{p,q,r}}{1}$.

Using the grade projection, we can extend the inner and outer products to general multivectors $X\_1, X\_2 \in \ga{p,q,r}$. The inner product for general multivectors is defined as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner_product}
    \grade{X_1}{k} \inner \grade{X_2}{l} = \grade{X_1X_2}{\vert k-l\vert },
\end{equation}
</div>

and the outer product as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:outer_product}
    \grade{X_1}{k} \outer \grade{X_2}{l} = \grade{X_1X_2}{k+l}.
\end{equation}
</div>

Also based on the geometric product, we can define other products, in particular the commutator product

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ga_commutator_product}
    X \times Y = \frac{1}{2}(XY - YX),
\end{equation}
</div>

and the anti-commutator product

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ga_anticommutator_product}
    X \bar{\times} Y = \frac{1}{2}(XY + YX).
\end{equation}
</div>

For non-degenerate geometric algebras, i.e. $r=0$, we can define the *dual* of a basis blade as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual_basis_blade}
    \gae{i}^* = \gae{i} I^{-1},
\end{equation}
</div>

where $I$ denotes the unit pseudoscalar of $\ga{p,q}$.

Later, for applying transformations to multivectors, we will need an operator called the *reverse* of a multivector. Let $X \in \ga{p,q,r}^k$ be a blade, i.e. $X = \bigwedge\_{i=1}^k \bm{x}\_i$, then the reverse of $X$, denoted as $\reverse{X}$, is defined as

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reverse_blade}
    \reverse{X} = \bigwedge_{i=k}^1 \bm{x}_i = (-1)^{k(k-1)/2}X.
\end{equation}
</div>

#### Example: 2D Geometric Algebra

In order to further motivate geometric algebra and to better explain the above definitions, consider the geometric algebra of the two-dimensional Euclidean plane. The underlying quadratic space is $\mathbb{R}^2$, which means it has two basis vectors $\gae{1}$ and $\gae{2}$ and both of them square to 1. The geometric algebra of this space is consequently denoted as $\ga{2}$, and its algebraic basis is

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:algebraic_basis_2dga}
    \bar{\mathbb{G}}_{2} = (1, \gae{1}, \gae{2}, \gae{12}).
\end{equation}
</div>

Clearly, the pseudoscalar of $\ga{2}$ is $I = \gae{12}$. Evaluating its square yields

<div markdown="0">
\begin{align}
    \gae{12}^2 & = \gae{12}\gae{12} = (\gae{1} \outer \gae{2})(\gae{1} \outer \gae{2})
    \\
    & = -\gae{12}\gae{21} = -(\gae{1} \outer \gae{2})(\gae{2} \outer \gae{1})
    \\
    & = -\gae{1}\gae{1}
    \\
    & = -1.
\end{align}
</div>

This is a very important result, because we have effectively introduced complex numbers simply by definition of a *real* geometric algebra. Other important isomorphisms can be found as $\ga{0} = \mathbb{R}, \ga{0,1} = \mathbb{C}, \ga{0,0,1} = \mathbb{D},$ and $\ga{0,2} = \mathbb{H}$.

#### Versors

For a non-degenerate geometric algebra $\ga{p,q}$, the set of multivectors constructed from the geometric product of $n\in \mathbb{N}^+$ invertible vectors is called the set of versors, i.e. $V = \prod\_{i=1}^{n} \bm{x}\_i$. Combined with the geometric product they form the Clifford group $\Gamma(p,q)$. The versors are invertible, i.e. $\versor\inverse{\versor} = 1$. When restricted to be of unit norm, we find the $Pin(p,q)$ group and the inverse becomes the reverse $\inverse{\versor} = \reverse{\versor}$. The subgroup when $n$ is even, is called the $Spin(p,q)$ group. The unit vectors $\bm{x}\_i$ that form the versors can be seen as hyperplanes in $\mathbb{R}^{p,q}$. Therefore, the $Pin(p,q)$ and $Spin(p,q)$ group represent arbitrary orthogonal transformations as product of vectors, since by the Cartan–Dieudonné theorem orthogonal transformations are found as multiple reflections in hyperplanes. More concretely, these groups are double covers of the Lie groups $O(p,q)$ and $SO(p,q)$, respectively, i.e. the orthogonal and special orthogonal groups. Transforming an arbitrary multivector $X \in \ga{p,q}$ is achieved via a sandwiching operation

<div markdown="0">
\begin{equation}\label{eq:versor_adjoint}
    Y = \versor X \reverse{\versor}.
\end{equation}
</div>

This operation is grade preserving, i.e. it leaves the number of $k$ basis vectors in outer product representation unchanged and thus does not change what the multivectors represent. Herein lies one of the advantages of geometric algebra, as it extends linear transformations naturally from vectors to the entire algebra, i.e. transformations can be applied to all multivectors in a uniform manner. They are a more general representation of rigid body transformations and can also be used to transform all geometric primitives that are part of the algebra and not just vectors. This extension is called outermorphism.
